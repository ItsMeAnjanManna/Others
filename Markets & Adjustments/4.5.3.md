# Section 4.5.3 – Differences Between Factor and Product Markets

This section explains the major differences between product markets (goods markets) and factor markets.

Remember:

- Product market = Market where finished goods and services are bought and sold.
- Factor market = Market where resources such as land, labour and capital are bought and sold.

The textbook identifies seven major differences.

## 1. Direct Demand vs Derived Demand

- Product market: Demand for consumer goods is called direct demand because consumers purchase goods to satisfy their needs.
- Factor market: Demand for factors of production is called derived demand because they are required to produce goods.

Example: People directly demand cars, but factories demand workers to manufacture those cars.

## 2. Who Creates the Demand?

- Product market: Demand is created by individual consumers.
- Factor market: Demand is created by firms involved in producing goods and services.

Example: Consumers create demand for clothes, while clothing factories create demand for workers and machines.

## 3. Determination of Equilibrium Price and Quantity

- Product market: Equilibrium price and quantity are determined by the interaction of demand and supply in a competitive market.
- Factor market: Equilibrium price and quantity of factors, such as labour, are determined by their demand and supply in a competitive factor market.

## 4. Factors Affecting Demand

Product market demand depends on:

- Price of the product.
- Prices of related goods.
- Consumer income.
- Taste and preferences.
- Income distribution.
- Demographic structure.

Factor market demand depends on:

- Marginal productivity (MP).
- Value of marginal product (VMP).
- Factor price.
- Availability of other cooperating factors.
- Production technology.

## 5. Price Elasticity of Demand

- Product market: Demand for non-durable goods is relatively price-inelastic, whereas demand for durable goods is relatively price-elastic.
- Factor market: Demand for scarce factors, such as specially skilled labour, is relatively price-inelastic because they cannot easily be substituted by other factors.

Example: A highly specialised surgeon cannot easily be replaced by an ordinary worker.

## 6. Price Elasticity of Supply

- Product market: Supply of non-durable or agricultural goods is relatively price-inelastic, whereas supply of durable consumer goods is relatively price-elastic.
- Factor market: Supply of scarce factors, such as capital in a less developed economy, may be relatively price-inelastic.

## 7. Final Goods vs Intermediate Goods

- Product market: Most consumer goods are final goods, meaning they are ready for final consumption.
- Factor market: Some factors of production are intermediate goods because they are used in further stages of production.

Example:

- Bread purchased by a consumer is a final good.
- Cotton yarn used to manufacture cotton clothes is an intermediate good.

## Quick Revision – Product Market vs Factor Market

| Basis               | Product Market            | Factor Market                            |
| ------------------- | ------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| Demand              | Direct demand             | Derived demand                           |
| Demand created by   | Consumers                 | Firms                                    |
| Price determination | Product demand and supply | Factor demand and supply                 |
| Demand factors      | Income, taste, prices     | Productivity, factor price               |
| Demand elasticity   | Depends on type of goods  | Depends on scarcity and substitutability |
| Supply elasticity   | Depends on type of goods  | Depends on availability of factors       |
| Goods               | Mostly final goods        | Factors and intermediate goods           |

## Important Conclusion

Although product markets and factor markets have several differences, they are closely interconnected.

Firms produce goods for consumers and simultaneously create demand for factors of production.

Therefore, expansion of the product market leads to expansion of the factor market.
