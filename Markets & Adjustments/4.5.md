# Section 4.5 – Difference Among Markets

This section explains how different markets are classified based on their structure and level of competition.

According to the textbook, markets can be distinguished on three main bases:

1. Degree of competitiveness.
2. Type of commodities traded.
3. Nature of control over the market.

## 4.5.1 Difference Due to Competitiveness
The level of competition depends on factors such as:

- Number of buyers and sellers.
- Control of individual sellers over market prices.
- Restrictions on entering or leaving the market.
- Availability of substitute products.

Based on these factors, markets are classified into the following types.

### A. Perfectly Competitive Market

- A large number of buyers and sellers exist.
- All sellers offer similar products.
- No individual seller can control the market price.

Example: Agricultural markets where many farmers sell similar products.

### B. Monopoly Market

- Only one seller or firm exists.
- There are no close substitutes for its product.
- The firm has significant control over market supply and price.
- Entry of new firms is restricted.

Examples mentioned in the textbook:

- CESC in electricity supply in Kolkata.
- Indian Railways in railway services.

### C. Price Discrimination under Monopoly

A monopolist charges different prices to different consumers for the same product or service.

Examples:

- A doctor charging different fees to rich and poor patients.
- Different electricity rates for domestic and commercial consumers.

### D. Monopolistic Competition

- A large number of firms exist.
- Firms sell differentiated products belonging to the same category.
- Products are close substitutes.
- Each firm has some control over its product price.

Example: Different toilet soap brands such as Lux, Dove, Nirma and Cinthol.

### E. Oligopoly Market

- A small number of firms dominate the market.
- Firms are interdependent, meaning one firm's decisions affect others.
- Entry of new firms is difficult.
- Firms may cooperate or compete with each other.

Example: Automobile manufacturing companies such as Tata Motors, Maruti Suzuki and Mahindra.

Types of oligopoly:

- Collusive oligopoly – Firms cooperate to influence prices or production.
- Non-collusive oligopoly – Firms compete independently.

### F. Bilateral Monopoly

A market where there is only one buyer and one seller.

Example: A labour market where a single trade union represents workers and a single employers' association represents employers.

### G. Monopsony

A market where there is only one buyer but potentially many sellers.

Examples mentioned in the textbook:

- Government of India as the single buyer of fighter aircraft in India.
- Government as the single buyer of warships.

## Quick Revision – Market Structures

| Market type              | Number of sellers | Key feature                              |
| ------------------------ | ----------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| Perfect competition      | Many              | No individual price control              |
| Monopoly                 | One               | Single seller                            |
| Price discrimination     | One               | Different prices for different consumers |
| Monopolistic competition | Many              | Differentiated products                  |
| Oligopoly                | Few               | Firms are interdependent                 |
| Bilateral monopoly       | One               | One buyer and one seller                 |
| Monopsony                | Many              | Single buyer                             |

Key takeaway: Different markets have different levels of competition depending on the number of buyers and sellers, product differences and control over prices.
