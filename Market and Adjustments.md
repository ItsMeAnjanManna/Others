# Chapter 4: Market and Adjustments

## Section 4.1 – Introduction

This section introduces the concept of a market economy and explains how markets developed as human society progressed.

### 1. How did the market economy develop?

To understand a market economy, we first need to understand how people lived and produced goods in earlier times.

Human society did not always have markets, shops, money or businesses. The economic system gradually developed through different stages.

Let's understand this with an example.

Imagine a group of people living in a village thousands of years ago.

Stage 1: People produced only what they needed

Initially, people depended on nature for their basic requirements, such as food, water and shelter.

They collected fruits, hunted animals and gathered other resources.

Their production was mainly for their own survival. There was very little exchange of goods.

Stage 2: People started producing more

Over time, people learned better methods of production.

For example:

- They learned agriculture.
- They started domesticating animals.
- They developed better tools.
- They learned to produce more food.

As a result, people sometimes produced more than they personally needed.

This extra production is called surplus production.

Stage 3: Division of labour and specialisation

As society developed, everyone no longer needed to perform every activity.

Different people started specialising in different occupations.

For example:

- Farmer – Produces rice and vegetables.
- Potter – Makes earthen pots.
- Carpenter – Makes furniture.
- Weaver – Produces clothes.

This is called division of labour.

When people concentrate on activities they are good at, their efficiency and productivity generally increase.

Stage 4: Exchange of goods

Now imagine a farmer who produces rice but needs clothes.

Instead of producing clothes himself, he exchanges some of his surplus rice with a weaver.

Similarly, the weaver exchanges clothes for rice.

This system of exchanging goods directly for other goods is called the barter system.

Stage 5: Development of the market economy

As the number of people, occupations and products increased, direct exchange became difficult.

For example, a farmer might need clothes but the weaver might not need rice.

To solve this problem, money gradually emerged as a common medium of exchange.

People could now sell their products for money and use that money to purchase whatever they needed.

Gradually, markets developed where buyers and sellers exchanged goods and services.

This gave rise to the modern market economy.

### 2. What is a market economy?

A market economy is an economic system in which the production, consumption and exchange of goods and services are largely guided by the forces of demand and supply.

Let's understand this with a simple example.

Suppose there is a market where people sell mangoes.

- If many people want mangoes but only a few mangoes are available, demand exceeds supply. Their prices may increase.
- If mangoes are available in large quantities but fewer people want to buy them, supply exceeds demand. Their prices may decrease.

Therefore, the forces of demand and supply influence the market price.

This is the basic idea behind a market economy.

### 3. What will we study in this chapter?

The introduction also tells us what the remaining chapter will cover.

There are three important areas:

A. Role of economic decision-makers

The chapter discusses three major decision-making units:

1. Households – They decide what to consume and how much labour to supply.
2. Firms – They decide what to produce and how much to produce.
3. Government – It makes policies, regulates markets and provides public goods and services.

B. Individual markets

Different products have different markets.

For example:

- Vegetable market
- Labour market
- Stock market
- Foreign exchange market
- Consumer goods market

The chapter explains how these markets operate and how they are connected.

C. Interlinking of markets

Different markets do not operate completely independently.

For example:

Suppose the demand for cars increases.

1. Car manufacturers increase production.
2. They need more steel, machinery and labour.
3. Demand for these inputs increases.
4. The factor markets are affected.

Thus, a change in one market can influence other markets.

# Section 4.2 – Evolution of Market Economy: The Price System

This section explains how the market economy evolved over time, starting from primitive human society and gradually developing into the modern industrial economy.

The main idea is that markets did not develop overnight. They evolved as humans learned new production methods, started specialising in different occupations, produced surplus goods and began exchanging products.

The textbook divides this evolution into three major stages:

1. Society in the pre-agricultural stage (hunting and food gathering).
2. A stage of settled cultivation.
3. Industrial Revolution and expansion of market.

Let's understand each stage separately.

## 4.2.1 Society in the pre-agricultural stage (Hunting and food gathering)

This was the earliest stage of human economic development.

During this period, people did not practise organised agriculture. They survived mainly by hunting animals and collecting naturally available food such as fruits, roots and plants.

The textbook identifies five important characteristics of this society.

1\. Production was mainly for survival

People collected only what they needed to satisfy their basic requirements.

Natural resources were sufficiently available compared to the demand for those resources.

For example, if a group needed food, they could collect fruits or hunt animals from the surrounding forest.

There was little need to produce extra goods.

2\. Very little competition or conflict

People had to move from one place to another to collect food.

However, there was relatively little struggle or conflict among people for acquiring food and other resources because natural resources were widely available.

3\. No private property

There was no well-developed concept of private ownership of property.

Resources were generally shared, and ownership attitudes were flexible.

The textbook relates this situation to the Marxist concept of primitive communism.

In simple words, people did not generally accumulate land, property or wealth individually.

4\. No importance of wealth accumulation

People consumed whatever they collected.

Since production was mainly for immediate survival, saving goods or accumulating individual wealth was not an important economic activity.

5\. Simple material culture

The number, quantity and variety of material goods were very limited.

People had only basic tools and products to fulfil their everyday requirements.

What should you understand from this stage?

Human society was mainly self-sufficient. People produced or collected goods for their own survival rather than producing goods for commercial exchange.

This was the starting point of economic development.

## 4.2.2 A stage of settled cultivation

As human knowledge and technology improved, people gradually moved away from their nomadic lifestyle and started living in permanent settlements.

This was an important turning point in economic development.

The textbook identifies several characteristics of this stage.

1\. People abandoned their nomadic lifestyle

Earlier, people continuously moved from one place to another in search of food.

Now, they started living in particular locations and practising agriculture.

For example, instead of searching for naturally available food every day, people started cultivating land and growing crops.

2\. Domestication of animals

People started keeping animals such as cows, goats and sheep.

These animals provided milk, meat, transportation and other useful resources.

3\. Development of private property

As people started settling in particular locations, the concept of private property gradually developed.

For example, a family could cultivate a particular piece of land and claim ownership over it.

4\. Scarcity of resources and the problem of choice

As society developed, people started recognising that resources were limited compared to their growing requirements.

This created the economic problem of scarcity and choice.

For example:

Suppose a farmer owns five acres of land. He can use the land to grow rice, wheat or vegetables.

But he cannot use the same five acres entirely for all three crops.

Therefore, he must decide how to allocate his limited resources.

This is an example of the economic problem of choice.

5\. Improvement in agricultural productivity

Gradually, agricultural production improved.

The textbook mentions two important types of productivity:

- Land productivity: How much agricultural output is produced per hectare of land.
- Labour productivity: How much output is produced by a worker.

For example:

Suppose two farmers cultivate one hectare of land.

- Farmer A produces 10 quintals of rice.
- Farmer B produces 20 quintals of rice.

Farmer B has higher land productivity.

Similarly, if one worker produces 10 units of output in a day and another produces 20 units, the second worker has higher labour productivity.

6\. Emergence of surplus food production

Due to improvements in agricultural productivity, farmers started producing more food than their families needed.

This extra production is called surplus production.

For example:

A farmer produces 100 kg of rice but his family needs only 60 kg.

The remaining 40 kg is surplus production.

7\. Development of the exchange economy and barter system

Farmers started exchanging their surplus products with other people.

For example:

- A farmer exchanges rice for clothes.
- A weaver exchanges clothes for vegetables.
- A potter exchanges pots for food.

This gradually developed into an exchange economy, also known as the barter economy.

8\. Emergence of new occupations

As agricultural production improved, everyone did not need to work as a farmer.

People started choosing different occupations.

For example:

- Artisans produced specialised goods.
- Priests performed religious activities.
- Soldiers provided protection.
- Government officials performed administrative activities.

This resulted in a social division of labour.

9\. Specialisation of labour

People started specialising in activities they could perform relatively better than others.

For example:

A carpenter became skilled in making furniture, while a farmer specialised in agricultural production.

This specialisation improved the efficiency of labour because people could concentrate on their respective areas of expertise.

10\. Increase in aggregate output

Due to specialisation and improved labour efficiency, the total production of goods and services increased.

In simple words, society could now produce more goods using its available resources.

### How did this stage help develop markets?

The textbook makes an important connection here.

As agricultural and labour productivity increased:

1. More goods became available in society.
2. Different people started earning income through different occupations.
3. People's purchasing power increased.
4. Demand for different goods and services increased.
5. Markets expanded.
6. Money gradually emerged as a common medium of exchange, making transactions easier.

Thus, improvements in agriculture and specialisation helped society move from a self-sufficient economy towards a market economy.

The textbook also mentions two important concepts:

- Absolute cost advantage: When a person or producer can produce a particular product using fewer inputs (such as labour hours) than another producer.
- Comparative cost advantage: When a person or producer can produce a product at a lower opportunity cost than another producer.

These advantages encourage specialisation and exchange between people.

## 4.2.3 Industrial Revolution and expansion of market

The third stage explains how the Industrial Revolution transformed an agricultural and subsistence economy into a modern industrial economy.

Before the Industrial Revolution, production was largely dependent on agriculture and manual labour.

However, technological inventions changed the entire production system.

The textbook identifies the period between 1760 and 1840 as the period of the Industrial Revolution's major development in Britain.

### 1. Introduction of new machines and technology

Several important inventions changed the way goods were produced.

For example:

- Steam engine
- Spinning Jenny
- Automatic looms

These inventions helped industries produce goods on a much larger scale.

Earlier, a worker might have produced a limited quantity of clothes manually.

With machines, factories could produce much larger quantities in less time.

### 2. Development of large-scale production

Industries started adopting factory-based production systems.

This allowed producers to manufacture goods in large quantities.

The textbook explains that industries could benefit from increasing returns to scale.

In simple language, increasing returns to scale means that when all inputs are increased by a certain proportion, output increases by a greater proportion.

For example:

If a factory doubles its inputs but its production becomes three times higher, it experiences increasing returns to scale.

### 3. Reduction in average cost of production

Large-scale production helped reduce the average cost of producing goods.

This gave manufacturers a price advantage.

They could sell their products at competitive prices while maintaining profitability.

### 4. Increase in employment opportunities

Industrial expansion created new employment opportunities.

People from rural areas started working in factories and industries.

This increased income and purchasing power, particularly among urban populations.

### 5. Development of the agricultural sector

Industrial development also helped agriculture.

For example, improved machinery and technology allowed farmers to increase agricultural productivity.

Large-scale farming could also benefit from economies of scale.

### 6. Expansion of markets

As people's income increased, their demand for different varieties of consumer goods also increased.

Consequently, markets expanded.

For example, people started demanding more clothes, household goods, machinery and other manufactured products.

This created a stronger relationship between industrial production, income and market expansion.

### 7. Different phases of the Industrial Revolution

Your textbook further divides industrial development into four major phases.

First Industrial Revolution (1760–1830)

- Mainly driven by coal-based energy.
- Steam engines became important.
- Textile industries experienced major technological improvements.

Second Industrial Revolution (1870–1968)

- Driven by electricity, gas and fossil fuels.
- Transportation became easier.
- Mechanical power dominated production systems.

Third Industrial Revolution (1969–1999)

- Emergence of nuclear energy.
- Development of electronics and computer technology.
- Increasing automation in industries.

Fourth Industrial Revolution (Since 2000)

- Development of the internet and renewable energy.
- Digital technology and real-time connectivity.
- Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Internet of Things (IoT).
- Large-scale data collection and analysis.

What is the importance of the Fourth Industrial Revolution?

Earlier, factories mainly depended on human labour and mechanical machines.

Today, factories can use AI, sensors and connected devices to monitor production, identify problems and improve efficiency.

For example, a modern automobile factory can use robots and connected sensors to monitor the manufacturing process.

This continuous technological development creates new products, services and markets.
